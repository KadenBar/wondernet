CHAPTER ONE

The Sky Goes Quiet

At the very edge of a sleepy little island stood a lighthouse with a blue door. Every evening, when the sea turned the color of blueberries, a girl named Pip climbed the spiral stairs to light its lantern.

Pip was not much taller than a stack of three dictionaries. The lantern, on the other hand, was taller than she was. It had a brass handle, seven glass windows, and a tiny bell that rang whenever the wind told a joke.

Usually, the stars appeared as soon as she opened the shutters. One winked above the harbor. Two twinkled over the cliffs. Hundreds more spilled across the sky like sugar across a table.

But one Thursday, the sky stayed dark. Pip waited. She counted to ten, then twenty, then all the way to ninety-nine. Not a single star blinked back.

She leaned out of the window. Far below, a boat captain called, “The sky has forgotten its freckles!”

“Then I suppose,” Pip said, tightening her scarf, “we had better help it remember.”

CHAPTER TWO

A Map of Missing Lights

Behind the lighthouse stove, Pip kept a map of every constellation she knew. There was the Dancing Spoon, the Long-Tailed Fox, and the Teapot That Never Poured. She spread the map across the floor and sat beside it with a pencil.

A soft knock came from under the door. It was Moss, the island's smallest hedgehog. He carried a pebble that glowed faintly in his paws.

“I found this beside the tide pools,” he said. “It hummed when I picked it up. Is that normal for rocks?”

Pip held the pebble to her ear. It made a sound like a lullaby heard through a wall. Inside the stone, a pinprick of light moved slowly from side to side.

“That isn't a rock,” she whispered. “It's a star that has forgotten how to shine.”

On the map, the Dancing Spoon faded until only its outline remained. Pip drew a circle around the tide pools. “If one star fell here, perhaps the others are close by.”

Moss tucked the pebble into his scarf. Together, they stepped into the moonless night.

CHAPTER THREE

The Fog Collector

The tide pools were covered in silver fog. Between the rocks sat a strange machine made of jars, fishing line, and bent spoons. Every jar held a little glow. Every glow was trembling.

Beside the machine stood a tall heron wearing spectacles. “Good evening,” he said. “Mind the wires. They are rather tangled.”

“Did you take the stars?” Pip asked.

“Borrowed,” said the heron. “My name is Hush. I was lonely. I thought if I put enough light in jars, I could have a night sky all to myself.”

Moss peered at the smallest jar. Its star was no brighter than a firefly. “They don't look very happy,” he said.

Hush sighed. “That's the trouble. In the sky, they sparkled. In my jars, they only shiver.” He adjusted his spectacles. “Perhaps light isn't something you can keep just by closing a lid.”

Pip looked at the frightened stars, then at the heron's long, drooping wings. “Maybe,” she said, “we can find a way for you to see them without keeping them.”

CHAPTER FOUR

A Small, Brave Spark

Pip climbed onto a rock and lifted the littlest jar. It was hardly bigger than an acorn. Inside it, one stubborn star refused to glow at all.

“You can go home,” she told it. “We won't leave you behind.”

She opened the lid. Nothing happened. Moss hummed the lullaby from his pebble. The star tilted toward the sound, as though it were listening.

Hush took off his spectacles and joined in. He had a voice like a creaky gate, but he sang every note carefully. Pip sang too, even though she didn't know all the words.

At first, the star shone only enough to light Pip's fingertips. Then it shone enough to reveal a tide pool. Then the whole beach lit up in gold, silver, and something very close to pink.

The star rose into the air. One by one, the other jars rattled as their stars leaped up to join it. They swirled above the cliffs like a flock of glowing birds.

“That's what they needed,” Hush said. “Not a smaller world. A bigger one.”

CHAPTER FIVE

Room for Every Star

By the time Pip and Moss reached the lighthouse, the Dancing Spoon was back in place. So were the Long-Tailed Fox and the Teapot That Never Poured. The sea glittered with their reflections.

Hush followed them to the blue door. “It is very beautiful,” he admitted. “But when the stars are so far away, won't I feel lonely again?”

Pip thought about this. Then she fetched three blankets, a pot of peppermint tea, and a small wooden bench. They carried everything up to the lighthouse roof.

“Here's an idea,” she said. “Every Thursday, we can meet here and watch the sky together. You won't need to keep the stars. And you won't need to watch them alone.”

Moss nodded. “I can bring biscuits. They might be mostly crumbs by the time I arrive.”

The heron laughed so hard his spectacles slipped down his beak. High above, a new constellation appeared. It looked suspiciously like three friends on a bench.

And from then on, the lighthouse lantern shone for anyone who needed a way home, while the stars shone for everyone.
